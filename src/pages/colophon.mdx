# Colophon

You're about to spend two weeks reading something, so it seems fair to say what
it is trying to do, what it deliberately doesn't do, and which parts are opinions
dressed up as facts.

Knowing why something was built the way it was tends to make it easier to read.

## Who it's for

Anyone who wants to understand how machine learning works and is willing to read
carefully for a couple of weeks. No programming experience is assumed. The code
is explained as it appears, and all of it runs in the page.

It was written by somebody who already writes software for a living, and that
shows up in one place only: a handful of asides comparing Python to other
languages, all of them folded shut and labelled. Nothing anywhere depends on
them. If they mean nothing to you, they cost you nothing.

The goal is not to make anyone a researcher. It's to leave you able to use this
material sensibly, know where each piece sits relative to the others, and read a
paper about it without drowning.

## Eight decisions

### 1. Results first, then mechanism

[Chapter 1](/learn/01-where-this-fits/) trains a working classifier in its first
eight lines, before defining a single term. Only then does it take the model
apart.

That's fastai's philosophy and I think it's right. The alternative — three
chapters of linear algebra before anything runs — loses people who would have been
perfectly capable, because **motivation is a resource and abstraction spends it**.

The rhythm everywhere here: show it working, then show why, then show what breaks.

### 2. Build it by hand, then throw it away

Chapters 8 and 9 build a neural network and its backward pass in NumPy, with
gradient checking. Chapter 10 replaces the lot with eleven lines of PyTorch.

That looks wasteful, and it's the most deliberate choice on the site.

You cannot debug a `nan` loss, a silently-zero gradient, or a layer that won't
learn if the library is a black box. Two days of derivation buys a year of not
guessing. People who skip those chapters don't write worse code — they write the
same code and then can't fix it.

### 3. Depth is available, never compulsory

The spine reads straight through without opening a single fold. Every derivation,
every "but why is it like that", every historical detour is behind a toggle.

This is the direct answer to *depth tied to relevance*. The reader who wants the
chain rule worked out symbol by symbol can have it. The reader who wants to know
what backpropagation **is** doesn't have to scroll past it.

### 4. The fortnight is a promise, so the extras exist

The single hardest constraint here was keeping two weeks to two weeks. A great
deal of lovely material doesn't fit.

So it lives in the [extras](/extras/) instead — fifteen full pieces, grouped by
*why* they exist: the deeper whys, the roads not taken, the things you only learn
by shipping, how we got here, and the field as a human activity.

<Aside type="key">
The rule that makes this work: **nothing in the sixteen chapters depends on any
extra.** Not one reference is load-bearing.

That's a promise in both directions. Skip every one and the path is complete.
Open them and you're not being handed filler to justify a longer table of
contents.
</Aside>

### 5. Classified asides, and named hard parts

Colour classifies; it never decorates. After one chapter you read the colour
without reading the label:

<Aside type="key">**Worth keeping** — the sentence to carry out of a section.</Aside>
<Aside type="nice">**Nice to know, not needed** — a sibling idea, a dead end, some history.</Aside>
<Aside type="rust">**You already know this one** — the bridge from what you have.</Aside>
<Aside type="warn">**Careful here** — a real failure mode, usually a silent one.</Aside>

Three components were added for this pass, and they come from David Perkins'
*Making Learning Whole*, which you sent:

**"If something isn't clicking"** boxes name the places people reliably get
stuck — in the reader's own words — and answer them. Perkins calls this *working
on the hard parts*, and his argument is that the sticking points are known in
advance, so pretending otherwise just means everyone trips privately and
concludes they're the stupid one.

**"Your turn"** boxes put the answer behind a fold, with a separate nudge before
it. The learning happens in the gap between reading the question and opening the
answer, and an answer in plain sight removes the gap.

**"Isn't that lovely"** boxes are the zoom-out — the connection to something far
away, the fact that lands, the reason somebody bothered. Never load-bearing,
always open, because you should stumble into them rather than go looking.

### 6. The map, not just a table of contents

A linear book has to pick an order, and any order hides the shape of the field.
[The map](/map/) is the antidote — 51 nodes, solid where this teaches them and
dashed where it doesn't, so the boundary of the fortnight is *visible* rather
than implied.

Its most important content is structural: three axes hang off machine learning
(*what feedback*, *what function shape*, *what fitting machinery*), and running
them together is the single largest source of early bewilderment. That's very
hard to say in a list and obvious in a tree.

### 7. Every code block runs, and every number is measured

Not screenshots. Not fences you copy elsewhere. **253 cells**, executable in
place, sharing state down the page like a notebook. Exercises have runnable
stubs and the solutions are folded beside them.

<Aside type="key">
And they're **checked**. `scripts/check_cells.py` extracts every runnable cell and
executes it in document order, in one namespace per file — exactly how you'd meet
them. It runs before every change lands.

This is not ceremony. Writing this thing, the checker caught: a diagnostic that
labelled underfitting as overfitting, a claimed parameter ratio that was off by a
factor of seventeen, a claimed speed-up that was 84× rather than "over a
hundred", an embedding demo where *guitar* and *rust* scored 0.996 similar, a
compression example whose own printed conclusion was false, and a demonstration of
RNN memory decay that demonstrated nothing at all.

Every number quoted in the prose is a number the code produced. Where a result is
weak, it says so — chapter 15's anomaly detector reports 14% detection at a 5%
false-alarm rate, because that's what it gets, and a friendlier example would have
taught you less.
</Aside>

### 8. It's allowed to be fun

The original draft was correct, dense and a bit cold. That's the default register
for technical writing and I don't think it's the right one for *teaching*.

So: a warmer voice throughout, 25 hand-drawn SVG illustrations, and a small
cast. Pip is the round thing that rolls downhill, and exists for a reason rather
than for decoration — rolling downhill is the algorithm the whole subject is
built on, so when Pip turns up on a slope in chapter 5, the joke and the lesson
are the same picture.

The other four are Wren, Basil, Juno and Moss, and each one is tied to exactly
one kind of box: something lovely, something to be careful about, something to
try, and somewhere people get stuck. After a chapter you stop reading the label
and recognise the face, which is the point of having faces.

<Aside type="nice">
The drawings are stroke-based SVG in `src/lib/doodles.ts`, and they all pass
through one filter: a `feTurbulence` plus `feDisplacementMap` that nudges every
path by a pixel or so.

That single filter is what makes geometrically exact shapes look hand-drawn — and,
more usefully, makes them all look drawn by the *same* hand. Twenty-five drawings,
one wobble.
</Aside>

## The notes for programmers

The site was written by a programmer, and there is a small amount of material
aimed at people in the same position — mostly comparisons between Python and
Rust, which is a language whose habits make Python surprising in specific ways.

Every one of those is a folded box labelled *if you already write code*, and
they appear only in the Python appendix and in a dozen chapter asides. They are
strictly additional. A reader who has never programmed can go from the first page
to the last without opening one, and the two-week path never refers back to
anything inside them.

They stay because they're useful to the people they're for, and folding them is
what makes it safe to keep them.

## Hover cards

Python constructs and mathematical results are underlined inline and open a card
on hover: a signature, one sentence, one example, and its output. Longer
explanations, including the programmer notes, are one click away on the appendix
pages.

The model is the documentation comment — most useful thing at the top, an example
immediately, a link to the real docs. Every card degrades to a plain link with
JavaScript off.

## How Python runs

Two backends behind one interface, because the two goals conflict.

**Browser (default).** Pyodide — CPython 3.14 compiled to WebAssembly — in a
module worker, vendored into `public/pyodide/` so it works offline *and* when
deployed. It ships numpy, pandas, scikit-learn, scipy, matplotlib and sympy, which
covers everything through hand-built neural networks. Roughly 70% of the
curriculum with **zero** prerequisites, for you and for anyone you send a link to.

**Local kernel.** A real Jupyter kernel over its standard websocket API, for the
PyTorch chapters. Full torch, full MPS, your own hardware.

<Reveal title="Why vendored rather than CDN, and why a module worker" kind="deep">

Both were bugs before they were decisions.

The first attempt loaded Pyodide from jsDelivr with `importScripts` in a classic
worker. It failed twice: some environments block cross-origin `importScripts`
inside a worker, and some browsers only permit *module* workers, where
`importScripts` doesn't exist at all. The fix — a module worker doing a dynamic
`import()` of `pyodide.mjs` — is also strictly better.

Vendoring followed the same logic. `scripts/vendor-pyodide.mjs` copies the core
from `node_modules` and downloads the wheel closure for the packages the
curriculum uses: 56 MB, gitignored, and after that the site needs no network at
all.

A CDN dependency in a local-first tutorial is a contradiction, and the failure
mode when it breaks is an opaque network error that teaches the reader nothing.
</Reveal>

## Design

Light only, by request. Warm cream paper, near-black ink, and a palette where
every hue is bound to one kind of content — teal for the path, amber for
sidetracks, rust for the notes aimed at programmers, violet for maths, berry for
*your turn*, sky for the moments of wonder.

That binding is what lets it be colourful and calm at once: you learn the palette
in one chapter and then read it at a glance forever. Nothing is coloured to be
pretty; it's pretty because it's consistent.

Body text is a serif at 18.5px over a 34rem measure, because you're going to read
it for a fortnight and a serif at a comfortable measure is kinder than a sans at
full width. Headings and UI use a rounded sans, which is most of where the
friendlier feel comes from.

Three columns on a wide screen — chapters, text, and what's on this page —
collapsing to one on a phone. The nav never reorders: the reading path is the
product.

<Aside type="nice">
The layout influence is [docs.fast.ai](https://docs.fast.ai), by your reference,
with the density lowered and the type given room.

The other influences are book-shaped rather than docs-shaped: *Crafting
Interpreters* for the build-it-then-explain-it rhythm, and the Rust Book for the
discipline of putting failure modes in the main text instead of a footnote.

The voice owes most to Eddie Woo — set up a pattern the reader already believes,
run it backwards until the surprising thing is forced, then step back and say
plainly what is nice about it. That is why chapter 9 asks *why right to left*
rather than asserting it, and why chapter 12 lets you notice that a one-hot times
a matrix is a row lookup instead of telling you.
</Aside>

## Sources

Three books, and it's worth being precise about which did what.

**Seth Weidman, *Deep Learning from Scratch* (O'Reilly, 2019).** The mathematics
and the from-scratch implementations were checked against this. Note that it's not
what its filename claimed — the file was labelled as the fastai book and contains
Weidman's. It's a good book and a better fit for these derivations than the label
would have been.

**Jeremy Howard and Sylvain Gugger, *Deep Learning for Coders with fastai and
PyTorch* (O'Reilly, 2020).** The teaching philosophy: top-down, get it working
first, theory when you need it. This borrows that and inverts it in places. The
[fastbook notebooks](https://github.com/fastai/fastbook) are free.

**David Perkins, *Making Learning Whole* (Jossey-Bass, 2009).** The structural
pass. Perkins' seven principles are the reason chapter 1 trains a real classifier
before defining a term (*play the whole game*), why the "if something isn't
clicking" boxes exist (*work on the hard parts*), why the map is at the front
(*uncover the hidden game*), and why chapter 16 is about learning without a
curriculum (*learn the game of learning*).

Specific results are cited where they appear: Cybenko and Hornik on universal
approximation, Vaswani et al. on attention, Kaplan et al. and Hoffmann et al. on
scaling, Belkin et al. on double descent, He et al. on residual connections.

## What was left out, and why

- **Reinforcement learning** as a chapter. A separate discipline with its own
  vocabulary and failure modes. Named, placed on the map, and given
  [a full extra](/extras/reinforcement-learning/) instead.
- **Proofs.** Convergence guarantees, VC dimension, PAC learning. Beautiful, and
  they won't change what you build.
- **Distributed training.** You'd need more than one GPU to follow it.
- **Prompt engineering.** It moves faster than any document can, and it's
  downstream of understanding what the model is doing.
- **Deployment tooling.** Docker, Kubernetes, model registries. Real subjects,
  and entirely about running software rather than about machine learning.

<Aside type="key">
The consistent rule: leave out anything you can pick up in an afternoon *once you
have the frame*, and keep anything that's hard to acquire later.

The frame is the deliverable. The details are search results.
</Aside>

## Where it's opinionated

Four claims here are judgements rather than facts, and you should know which.

**"Gradient boosting usually beats neural networks on tabular data."** Well
supported, not universal, contested at the margins — and the margin moves.

**"Build it by hand first."** Plenty of excellent practitioners went top-down and
never wrote a backward pass. The argument is debuggability. It's an argument, not
a proof.

**"Frame the problem before you model."** The least controversial and the most
ignored.

**"Depth is exponentially more efficient than width."** Empirical, well-evidenced,
and not a theorem. The universal approximation theorem says the opposite is
*possible*; the field went deep because it kept working.

## Corrections

it's wrong in a file you can edit: chapters in `src/content/chapters/`, extras in
`src/content/extras/`, appendices in `src/content/py/` and `src/content/math/`,
the map in `src/lib/conceptmap.ts`, and the drawings in `src/lib/doodles.ts`.
