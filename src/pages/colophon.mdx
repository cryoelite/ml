# Colophon

You are about to spend two weeks reading something. It seems only fair to tell
you what it is trying to do, what it decided not to do, and where it is built on
opinions rather than facts.

## The brief

One reader: a backend engineer who writes Rust, can pick up Python, and wants to
learn machine learning properly in a fortnight — not to build new architectures,
but to use the field effectively, know where every piece sits, and be able to
read a paper without drowning.

Everything below follows from that.

## Six decisions

### 1. Results first, then mechanism

[Chapter 1](/learn/01-where-this-fits/) trains a working classifier in its first
eight lines, before defining a single term. Only then does it take the model
apart.

This is fastai's philosophy and it is right. The alternative — three chapters of
linear algebra before anything runs — loses people who would have been perfectly
capable, because motivation is a resource and abstraction spends it. The
compromise everywhere here: **show it working, then show why, then show what
breaks.**

### 2. Build it by hand, then throw it away

Chapters 8 and 9 build a neural network and its backward pass in NumPy, with
gradient checking. Chapter 10 replaces the lot with eleven lines of PyTorch.

That looks wasteful and it is the most deliberate choice on the site. You cannot
debug `nan` loss, a silently-zero gradient, or a layer that will not learn if the
library is a black box. Two days of derivation buys a year of not guessing.

### 3. Depth is available, never compulsory

The spine is meant to read straight through without opening a single collapsed
section. Every derivation, every "but why is it like that", every historical
detour is folded away behind a **derivation** or **deep dive** toggle.

This is the direct answer to *depth tied to relevance*. The reader who wants the
chain rule worked out symbol by symbol can have it; the reader who wants to know
what backpropagation *is* does not have to scroll past it.

### 4. Classified asides

Colour is used to classify, never to decorate. Five kinds, and they are load-bearing:

<Aside type="key">**Worth remembering** — the sentence to keep from a section.</Aside>
<Aside type="nice">**Nice to know** — a sibling idea, a dead end, some history. Skippable on purpose.</Aside>
<Aside type="rust">**Coming from Rust** — the bridge to what you already know.</Aside>
<Aside type="warn">**Where people get hurt** — a real failure mode, usually a silent one.</Aside>

The point of the second one is the *nice to know* tier you asked for: things that
exist, that you can leave without, and that you now have a name for.

### 5. The map, not just the table of contents

A linear book has to pick an order, and any order hides the shape of the field.
[The map](/map/) is the antidote — 51 nodes, solid where the tutorial teaches
them and dashed where it does not, so the boundary of the two weeks is visible
rather than implied.

Its most important content is structural: three axes hang off machine learning
(*what feedback*, *what function shape*, *what fitting machinery*) and conflating
them is the single largest source of early confusion. That is very hard to say in
a list and obvious in a tree.

### 6. Every code block runs

Not a screenshot, not a fence you copy elsewhere. 177 cells, executable in place,
sharing state down the page like a notebook. The exercises have runnable stubs
and the solutions are collapsed beside them.

<Aside type="key">
And they are **checked**. `scripts/check_cells.py` extracts every runnable cell
and executes it in document order in one namespace per chapter — which is exactly
how you would meet them. It is run before every change lands. Several claims in
these chapters were wrong until it said so, and the numbers in the prose are the
numbers the code actually produced.
</Aside>

## The Rust bridge

You know an ownership model, a type system and an iterator API. Those are exactly
the concepts that make Python confusing, because Python looks similar and behaves
differently.

So the Python appendix carries, for each entry, *how a Rust programmer already
thinks about this* — an **analogy, not a translation**. Half of these have no Rust
equivalent (broadcasting, `**kwargs`, decorators, autograd) and pretending
otherwise would mislead you. Where the honest answer is "there isn't one", the
entry says so.

The four differences that recur, and that cost an hour each if nobody says them:
no ownership, no `Result`, annotations are not types, and NumPy slices alias like
`&mut` with no borrow checker to stop you.

## Hover cards

Python constructs and mathematical results are underlined inline and open a card
on hover with a signature, one sentence, one example, its output, and the Rust
note. Longer explanations live on the appendix pages, one click away.

The model is the documentation comment: the most useful thing at the top, an
example immediately, and a link to the real docs — [docs.python.org](https://docs.python.org/3.14/)
for stdlib, the library's own reference otherwise. Every card degrades to a plain
link with JavaScript off.

## How Python runs

Two backends behind one interface, because the two goals conflict.

**Browser (default).** Pyodide — CPython 3.14 compiled to WebAssembly — in a
module worker, vendored into `public/pyodide/` so it works offline and when
deployed. It ships numpy, pandas, scikit-learn, scipy, matplotlib and sympy,
which covers everything through hand-built neural networks. That is roughly 70%
of the curriculum with **zero** prerequisites, for you and for anyone you send a
link to.

**Local kernel.** A real Jupyter kernel over its standard websocket API, for the
PyTorch chapters. Full torch, full MPS, your own hardware.

<Reveal title="Why vendored rather than CDN, and why a module worker" kind="deep">

Both were bugs before they were decisions.

The first attempt loaded Pyodide from jsDelivr with `importScripts` in a classic
worker. It failed twice: some environments block cross-origin `importScripts`
inside a worker, and some browsers only permit *module* workers, where
`importScripts` does not exist at all. The fix — a module worker doing a dynamic
`import()` of `pyodide.mjs` — is also strictly better.

Vendoring followed the same logic. `scripts/vendor-pyodide.mjs` copies the core
from `node_modules` and downloads the wheel closure for the packages the
curriculum uses: 56 MB, gitignored, and after that the site needs no network at
all. A CDN dependency in a local-first tutorial is a contradiction, and the
failure mode when it breaks is an opaque network error that teaches the reader
nothing.
</Reveal>

## Design

Light only, by request. Warm off-white paper, near-black ink, one accent
(a deep teal) with amber, rust and violet reserved for the aside classes. Body
text is a serif at 18.5px over a 34rem measure, because you are going to read it
for two weeks and a serif at a comfortable measure is kinder than a sans at full
width.

Three columns on a wide screen — chapters, text, and what is on this page —
collapsing to one on a phone. The nav never reorders: the reading path is the
product.

<Aside type="nice">
The influence is [docs.fast.ai](https://docs.fast.ai), by your reference, with the
density lowered and the type given room. The other influences are book-shaped
rather than docs-shaped: *Crafting Interpreters* for the "build it and then
explain it" rhythm, and the Rust Book for the discipline of putting the failure
modes in the main text instead of a footnote.
</Aside>

## Sources

The mathematics and the from-scratch implementations were checked against **Seth
Weidman, *Deep Learning from Scratch: Building with Python from First Principles*
(O'Reilly, 2019)**, which is what you actually attached — the file was labelled as
the fastai book but contains Weidman's. It is a good book and a better fit for the
derivations here than the label would have been.

The teaching philosophy is **fastai's** — Howard and Gugger, *Deep Learning for
Coders with fastai and PyTorch* — whose top-down, get-it-working-first approach
this borrows and inverts in places. The [fastbook notebooks](https://github.com/fastai/fastbook)
are free.

Specific results are cited where they appear: Cybenko and Hornik on universal
approximation, Vaswani et al. on attention, Kaplan et al. and Hoffmann et al. on
scaling, Belkin et al. on double descent.

## What was left out, and why

- **Reinforcement learning.** A separate discipline with its own vocabulary and
  failure modes. Two weeks cannot hold both. Named and placed on the map.
- **Proofs.** Convergence guarantees, VC dimension, PAC learning. Beautiful, and
  they will not change what you build.
- **A distributed training chapter.** You would need more than one GPU to follow
  it.
- **Prompt engineering.** It moves faster than any document can, and it is
  downstream of understanding what the model is doing.
- **Deployment tooling.** Docker, Kubernetes, model registries. You are a backend
  engineer; a model is a function, and you already know how to ship functions.

<Aside type="key">
The consistent rule: leave out anything you can pick up in an afternoon *once you
have the frame*, and keep anything that is hard to acquire later. The frame is
the deliverable. The details are search results.
</Aside>

## Where it is opinionated

Three claims here are judgements rather than facts, and you should know which.

**"Gradient boosting usually beats neural networks on tabular data."** Well
supported and not universal. Contested at the margins, and the margin moves.

**"Build it by hand first."** Plenty of excellent practitioners went top-down and
never wrote a backward pass. The argument for doing it is debuggability, and it
is an argument, not a proof.

**"Frame the problem before you model."** The least controversial and the most
ignored.

## Corrections

The cell checker catches broken code, not wrong prose. If a claim here is wrong,
it is wrong in a file you can edit: chapters are in
`src/content/chapters/*.mdx`, the appendices in `src/content/py/` and
`src/content/math/`, and the map in `src/lib/conceptmap.ts`.
