---
title: 'stacking and concatenating'
lib: 'numpy'
summary: 'Join arrays along an existing axis (concatenate) or along a brand new one (stack). Confusing them is a shape bug.'
signature: 'np.concatenate([a, b], axis=0)   |   np.stack([a, b], axis=0)'
example: |-
  a, b = np.ones((2, 3)), np.zeros((2, 3))
  np.concatenate([a, b], axis=0).shape, np.stack([a, b]).shape
output: '((4, 3), (2, 2, 3))'
rust: '`concat` is `a.extend(b)` on the outer dimension. `stack` is collecting into a `Vec<[[f64; 3]; 2]>` — one more level of nesting than you started with.'
docs: 'https://numpy.org/doc/stable/reference/generated/numpy.stack.html'
docsLabel: 'numpy.stack ↗'
---

The distinction, once:

- **`concatenate`** joins along an axis that already exists. Two `(2, 3)` arrays
  become one `(4, 3)`. The number of dimensions does not change.
- **`stack`** creates a new axis. Two `(2, 3)` arrays become `(2, 2, 3)`. The
  number of dimensions goes up by one.

`np.column_stack` is the convenience you want when building a design matrix — it
takes a list of 1-D columns and gives you `(n, k)`, which is what
[chapter 4's bias trick](/learn/04-your-first-model/) uses. `np.hstack` and
`np.vstack` are older spellings that guess the axis for you, and the guessing is
why people prefer the explicit versions.

PyTorch calls them `torch.cat` and `torch.stack`, with identical semantics and a
shorter name for the one you use more.
