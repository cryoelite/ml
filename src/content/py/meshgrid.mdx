lib: 'numpy'
summary: 'Turns two 1-D ranges into a full 2-D grid of every combination, so you can evaluate a function everywhere at once.'
signature: 'X, Y = np.meshgrid(xs, ys)'
example: |-
  X, Y = np.meshgrid([0, 1, 2], [10, 20])
  X.shape, (X + Y)
output: '((2, 3), array([[10, 11, 12], [20, 21, 22]]))'
rust: '`xs.iter().cartesian_product(ys)`, but returned as two parallel 2-D arrays instead of a stream of pairs — so you can do arithmetic on all combinations at once.'
docs: 'https://numpy.org/doc/stable/reference/generated/numpy.meshgrid.html'
docsLabel: 'numpy.meshgrid ↗'
---

Two 1-D arrays in, two 2-D arrays out, such that `X[i, j], Y[i, j]` is one
combination. Doing arithmetic on `X` and `Y` then evaluates your function at
every point of the grid, with no loop.

It is how every contour plot and decision-boundary picture in this tutorial is
made: build a grid over the input space, run the model on all of it, reshape the
predictions back to the grid's shape, and hand the result to `contourf`.

The one gotcha is ordering. By default the first argument varies along the
*columns*, which is the opposite of what matrix intuition suggests. If your plot
comes out transposed, that is why — `indexing="ij"` swaps it to matrix
convention.
