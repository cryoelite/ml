---
title: 'broadcasting'
lib: 'numpy'
summary: 'Arrays of different shapes are stretched to a common shape before an elementwise operation, without copying.'
signature: '(3, 1) op (1, 4)  ->  (3, 4)'
example: |-
  a = np.arange(3).reshape(3, 1)   # (3,1)
  b = np.arange(4)                 # (4,)
  (a + b).shape
output: '(3, 4)'
rust: 'No equivalent. The nearest thing is writing the nested loop yourself and indexing one operand with a stride of zero — which is literally what NumPy does under the hood.'
docs: 'https://numpy.org/doc/stable/user/basics.broadcasting.html'
docsLabel: 'Broadcasting rules ↗'
---

The rule, applied right to left: two dimensions are compatible if they are
equal, or if one of them is `1`. A dimension of `1` is repeated as many times
as needed. Missing leading dimensions are treated as `1`.

So `(32, 10) + (10,)` works and adds the same ten numbers to every one of the
32 rows — which is exactly how a bias vector is added to a batch of
activations. You will write that line, or a line PyTorch writes for you, in
every neural network you ever build.

It is also the most common source of silent bugs. `(32, 1) * (1, 32)` does not
fail; it produces a `(32, 32)` matrix, and if you expected 32 numbers you now
have 1024 and a metric that quietly means nothing. When something is off by an
order of magnitude, print `.shape` first.
