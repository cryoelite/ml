Cross-entropy splits cleanly into two parts:

$$H(p, q) = H(p) + D_{KL}(p \parallel q)$$

The first term is fixed by the data. The second is yours. So **minimising
cross-entropy is exactly minimising the KL divergence between your model and
reality** — which is the justification for the loss you have been using since
chapter 4.

It is *not* a distance, whatever it feels like: $D_{KL}(p \parallel q)$ and
$D_{KL}(q \parallel p)$ are different numbers, and the asymmetry has real
consequences.

Minimising the forward direction is **mode-covering** — $q$ must put mass
everywhere $p$ does, or the $\log(p/q)$ term blows up — so the model hedges and
spreads out. Minimising the reverse is **mode-seeking**: $q$ can safely ignore
parts of $p$, so it picks one mode and commits sharply.

That is the technical root of why VAEs blur and GANs do not, and why RLHF's KL
penalty keeps a tuned model close to its starting point rather than letting it
drift into whatever maximises the reward model.
