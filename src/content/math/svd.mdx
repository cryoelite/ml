Every matrix, of any shape, factors this way. That is a surprisingly strong
statement: whatever linear map $A$ performs, it can always be decomposed into
*rotate, stretch along axes, rotate again*.

The singular values in $\Sigma$ say how much stretching happens in each
direction, sorted largest first. Keep only the top $k$ and you get the **best
possible rank-$k$ approximation** of the matrix, in the least-squares sense — a
result strong enough to have a name (Eckart–Young).

That is why SVD is everywhere:

- **PCA** is the SVD of your centred data. The rows of $V^\top$ are the principal
  components, and the singular values squared are the variance explained.
- **Low-rank approximation** — image compression, and the "LoRA" fine-tuning that
  trains a small rank-$r$ update instead of a full weight matrix.
- **Pseudo-inverse** — the numerically stable way to solve least squares when the
  normal equations would be singular.

In practice always reach for `np.linalg.svd` over forming $X^\top X$ and
inverting it. Squaring the matrix squares its condition number, which is a fast
way to lose precision you did not have to lose.
