Read the formula as a picture: pick any two points on the curve, draw the straight
line between them, and the function stays underneath it. A bowl is convex; a
landscape with hills and hollows is not.

The consequence is the whole reason anyone cares: **a convex function has exactly
one minimum, and any downhill method finds it.** No local traps, no dependence on
where you started, and often a provable convergence rate.

Squared error with a linear model is convex. So is logistic regression's loss, and
an SVM's. Adding an L2 penalty makes a convex problem *strictly* convex, which
also makes the solution unique.

Neural networks are wildly non-convex, and for years that was a serious and
respectable objection to them — you could prove nothing. What changed was not the
mathematics but the empirical discovery that in very high dimensions the
non-convexity mostly does not bite: critical points are overwhelmingly saddles
rather than minima, and the minima you do reach are about equally good.

That story is the [gradient descent extra](/extras/why-gradient-descent-works/),
and it is a good example of a field advancing by measurement when theory could
not follow.
