Most vectors get rotated when you multiply them by a matrix. Eigenvectors do not
— they come out pointing the same way, only longer or shorter, and the eigenvalue
is by how much.

The reason this matters here is what happens when you apply the same matrix
repeatedly. After $n$ steps, components along an eigenvector have been multiplied
by $\lambda^n$. So:

- $|\lambda| < 1$ → that component **decays geometrically** toward nothing
- $|\lambda| > 1$ → it **explodes geometrically**
- $|\lambda| = 1$ → it survives

The largest $|\lambda|$ is called the **spectral radius**, and it decides the
whole system's fate. That is precisely why an RNN's gradient
[vanishes or explodes](/extras/before-transformers/) depending on the recurrent
matrix's spectral radius, and why careful weight initialisation aims to keep the
relevant scale near 1.

They also underlie PCA (the principal components are eigenvectors of the
covariance matrix), and the curvature of a loss surface — the Hessian's
eigenvalues tell you which directions are steep and which are flat, which is the
`w`-fast, `b`-slow problem from chapter 5 stated precisely.
