Take logs of both sides and a power law becomes a straight line. That is why
scaling plots are always drawn on log-log axes, and why a straight line on such a
plot is the signature you are looking for.

The empirical finding — and it genuinely was not obvious in advance — is that
language model loss follows one over roughly **nine orders of magnitude** of
compute. Most engineering relationships hit a knee, a wall, or a regime change.
This one did not.

The practical consequence reshaped an industry: **you can run small experiments
and predict the loss of a model a thousand times larger**, before committing the
money. That turned frontier training from a gamble into a capital allocation
decision somebody could defend in a spreadsheet.

Two cautions. The small exponent is the sting: $\alpha \approx 0.07$ means a
**tenfold** increase in compute buys only about a 15% reduction in the reducible
loss. Progress is real, predictable, and extremely expensive.

And loss is not capability. The loss curve is smooth; the behaviours people care
about sometimes appear abruptly, and how much of that abruptness is real versus
an artefact of discontinuous metrics is an active and unresolved argument.
